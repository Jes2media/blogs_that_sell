You asked ChatGPT to write your sales page. It did—in 30 seconds.

The copy is grammatically perfect. It hits all the structural beats. It even sounds vaguely professional. But something's off.

It reads like it was written by a very smart person who has never actually sold anything.

---

## The Generic Copy Problem

AI copy has a specific flavor. Once you notice it, you can't unsee it:

- **Corporate hedging:** "This comprehensive solution can potentially help businesses achieve better results."
- **Empty superlatives:** "Amazing," "incredible," "revolutionary," "game-changing."
- **Symmetrical lists:** Everything comes in threes. Every benefit has a parallel structure.
- **No stakes:** Problems are described clinically, without emotional weight.
- **Missing voice:** Could have been written by anyone, for anyone, about anything.

The copy is *correct*. It's just not *compelling*.

This isn't a minor style issue. Generic copy tanks conversions. When readers feel like they're reading a template, they trust you less—and trust is what makes people buy.

---

## Why AI Writes This Way

Understanding why AI produces generic copy helps you fix it:

### 1. AI optimizes for "average good"

Large language models are trained on massive datasets of existing content. They learn what "good writing" looks like by averaging across millions of examples.

The result is writing that's statistically probable—the most likely next word, the most common phrase structures, the safest rhetorical choices.

Safe doesn't sell. Distinctive does.

### 2. AI has no skin in the game

When you write copy for your own business, you know:
- The exact objections your customers raise
- The specific language they use to describe their problems
- The stories that made past customers say yes
- What differentiates you from competitors who look similar

AI knows none of this. It's guessing at a generic version of your customer, your offer, and your market position.

### 3. AI can't feel the stakes

Good copy has tension. The reader needs to feel that something real is at risk—that their problem won't solve itself, that inaction has costs, that this decision matters.

AI describes problems. It doesn't feel them. So its copy reads like a Wikipedia entry about pain rather than a mirror reflecting the reader's actual experience.

### 4. AI defaults to balance

Human copywriters know when to be unbalanced—when to spend 80% of the page on the problem and 20% on the solution, or when to lead with a provocative claim instead of a safe one.

AI tends toward equilibrium. It gives equal weight to everything, which means nothing feels urgent or important.

## The Real Problem: Using AI as a Writer Instead of a Starting Point

Here's where most people go wrong: they treat AI output as a first draft that needs light editing.

It's not.

AI output is **raw material**—closer to an outline or a brainstorm than a draft. The transformation from generic AI copy to persuasive human copy isn't editing. It's rewriting with your brain fully engaged.

The people getting results from AI copywriting aren't using it to write. They're using it to think faster, then doing the actual writing themselves.

---

## The Transformation Process: Generic to Compelling

Here's how to take AI output and make it actually convert:

### Step 1: Strip the generic opening

AI loves to start with context-setting that delays the point:

> "In today's fast-paced digital landscape, businesses face increasing pressure to stand out. Content marketing has become essential for reaching target audiences and building brand awareness."

This could introduce literally any marketing product or service. It tells readers nothing and earns no attention.

**Fix:** Start with the specific problem your specific reader has right now.

> "Your blog gets traffic. It doesn't get leads. You've published 50 posts and your email list has grown by 12 people."

Same product, completely different energy. The reader either recognizes themselves or doesn't—either way, you've earned their attention.

### Step 2: Replace adjectives with evidence

AI loves empty modifiers: "powerful," "effective," "proven," "cutting-edge," "comprehensive."

These words mean nothing without evidence. In fact, they often signal that you have no evidence.

**The rule:** Every adjective needs to either be cut or backed up.

| Generic | Specific |
|---------|----------|
| "Powerful email templates" | "Email templates with 42% average open rates" |
| "Proven framework" | "The framework I used to generate $127K from a 12-post blog" |
| "Comprehensive guide" | "47-page guide covering all 12 stages of the launch" |

The specific version might be less impressive-sounding. It's more believable—and believable converts.

### Step 3: Add your actual voice

AI writes in a voice that belongs to no one. Your copy should sound like you wrote it.

**The test:** Read your copy aloud. Does it sound like something you'd say to a smart friend who asked about your work?

If it sounds like a press release or a LinkedIn post from a brand account, it's not your voice yet.

Voice markers that AI strips out:
- **Opinions:** "Honestly, most email courses are garbage."
- **Specific references:** "I learned this the hard way after my 2019 launch flopped."
- **Casual language:** "Here's the thing..." or "Look..."
- **Direct address:** "You're probably wondering if this will work for your situation."

These feel risky because they're personal. That's exactly why they work.

### Step 4: Make the stakes real

AI describes problems clinically:

> "Many businesses struggle with lead generation and find it challenging to convert website visitors into customers."

There's no urgency here. The problem sounds like a mild inconvenience.

**Fix:** Make the reader *feel* what the problem costs them.

> "Every day your website gets traffic that doesn't convert, you're paying for visitors who leave with nothing—and they're not coming back. That 2% conversion rate isn't a metric. It's money walking out the door."

Same problem. Now it has weight.

### Step 5: Cut the symmetry

AI loves balanced structures:

> "You'll learn how to:
> - Create compelling content that resonates
> - Build authentic relationships that convert
> - Develop strategic systems that scale"

This is so balanced it creates no hierarchy. Everything feels equally important, which means nothing feels urgent.

**Fix:** Lead with your strongest point. Make it concrete. Let the structure be uneven.

> "You'll walk away knowing exactly how to turn a single blog post into 20+ email subscribers—every time you publish. You'll also learn how I structure content for sharing, and the follow-up sequence that converts readers to buyers."

Less elegant. More useful. The reader knows exactly what they're getting.

### Step 6: Add real proof

AI invents generic social proof:

> "Trusted by thousands of businesses worldwide."

This is the copywriting equivalent of "nice personality." It means nothing.

**Fix:** Specific proof, even if it's smaller.

> "Used by 847 freelancers and consultants since 2022. Here's what happened when Amanda K. applied this to her coaching business..."

847 is less impressive than "thousands." It's also believable—and that makes it more persuasive.

## What AI Is Actually Good For

AI isn't useless for copywriting. It's just not useful in the way most people use it.

**Use AI for:**

- **Generating options:** "Give me 10 different angles for this headline" gets you thinking, even if all 10 are mediocre.
- **Overcoming blank page syndrome:** Starting from something is easier than starting from nothing.
- **Structural scaffolding:** AI can outline a sales page structure that you then rewrite with actual persuasion.
- **Identifying gaps:** "What objections might someone have to this offer?" produces a useful list to address.
- **Variation testing:** "Rewrite this CTA 5 different ways" gives you A/B test candidates.

**Don't use AI for:**

- **Final copy:** Ever. Not without substantial rewriting.
- **Voice:** AI can't capture what makes you distinctive.
- **Strategy:** AI doesn't know your market, customers, or competitive position.
- **Emotional resonance:** AI describes feelings. It doesn't create them.

The 10x productivity promise of AI copywriting is real—but only if you understand that the work has shifted from writing to editing, not disappeared.

## The Editing Checklist: Before You Publish AI-Assisted Copy

Before any AI-assisted copy goes live, run it through these checks:

**Voice Check**
- [ ] Does this sound like me or like a robot?
- [ ] Have I added opinions, stories, or specific references?
- [ ] Would I say this to a customer in person?

**Specificity Check**
- [ ] Are all claims backed with evidence or examples?
- [ ] Have I replaced generic adjectives with concrete details?
- [ ] Is the problem description specific to my actual customers?

**Stakes Check**
- [ ] Does the reader feel what's at risk?
- [ ] Is there urgency beyond artificial deadlines?
- [ ] Have I made inaction feel costly?

**Trust Check**
- [ ] Is my proof specific and verifiable?
- [ ] Have I addressed the objections my real customers raise?
- [ ] Does anything sound too good to be true?

If you can't check all of these, you're publishing generic copy that will underperform. The AI saved you time—but you're leaving money on the table.

---

## The Bottom Line

AI is a tool, not a replacement.

The copywriters and marketers getting results with AI aren't using it to write. They're using it to accelerate the thinking and outlining process, then applying human judgment, voice, and market knowledge to the output.

Generic AI copy converts poorly because readers recognize it—consciously or not—as something that wasn't written for them specifically. And copy that feels generic gets treated as generic: skimmed, forgotten, unconverted.

The fix isn't better prompts. It's accepting that the work hasn't disappeared—it's shifted from generation to transformation. The blank page problem is solved. The persuasion problem is still yours.

If you want copy that converts, you still need to know what makes copy convert. AI can't learn that for you.
