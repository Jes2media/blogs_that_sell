PAS—Problem, Agitate, Solution—is a three-step persuasion framework that names the reader's pain, intensifies it, then presents a resolution. It's the most taught copywriting formula in direct response because it's simple enough to remember, flexible enough to apply anywhere, and effective enough that professionals still use it after fifty years.

## Who Created PAS

The exact origin is murky. Unlike AIDA, which traces back to E. St. Elmo Lewis in 1898, PAS emerged organically from direct mail practice rather than academic theory.

**Dan Kennedy** is most responsible for codifying and popularizing it. Through his books, seminars, and GKIC (Glazer-Kennedy Insider Circle), Kennedy drilled PAS into a generation of direct-response copywriters. He didn't claim to invent it—he positioned it as inherited wisdom from the mail-order tradition.

**Gary Halbert** used PAS structure constantly without naming it. His famous "coat of arms" letter opens with problem (you don't know your family heritage), agitates (your kids will never know where they came from), then solves (we've researched your family crest). Pure PAS, never labeled.

**John Carlton** became known for aggressive agitation—the "twist the knife" approach. His golf and fitness ads are case studies in how far you can push the middle step without losing credibility.

**Ray Edwards** extended PAS into PASTOR—adding Story, Testimony, Offer, and Response. His contribution was recognizing that the three steps work best as a foundation, not a complete structure.

**Joanna Wiebe** brought PAS into conversion copywriting for software and SaaS. Her work at Copy Hackers showed that PAS isn't just for long-form sales letters—it works in email subject lines, landing pages, and even button copy.

## How PAS Works

### Step 1: Problem

State the specific problem your reader faces. Not a category of problems—their actual, felt problem.

The key word is *felt*. You're not describing an objective situation. You're naming what the reader experiences.

**Weak problem statement:**
> "Many businesses struggle with lead generation."

**Strong problem statement:**
> "You've published 47 blog posts this year. Your traffic is up. But your calendar is empty. No calls booked. No leads in the pipeline. Just content feeding a void."

The difference: specificity and ownership. "You" not "many businesses." Numbers not generalities. Symptoms not abstractions.

Dan Kennedy teaches that you should be able to describe the reader's problem better than they can describe it themselves. When you do, they unconsciously assume you also have the solution.

### Step 2: Agitate

Make the problem hurt. Show the consequences, costs, and compounding effects of leaving it unsolved.

This is where copywriters diverge. Kennedy keeps agitation logical—costs, opportunity costs, competitive disadvantage. Carlton goes emotional—humiliation, frustration, the feeling of being left behind. Both work. Know your audience.

**Logical agitation:**
> "Every month without a working lead system, you're not just missing revenue—you're paying for it. The content costs money. The tools cost money. Your time costs money. And the compounding effect means you're falling further behind competitors who fixed this a year ago."

**Emotional agitation:**
> "You see other consultants in your space closing deals. Same credentials. Sometimes less experience. But they figured out something you haven't. And the longer you go without solving it, the more the gap widens. Six months from now, twelve months from now—where does this trajectory end?"

The purpose of agitation isn't manipulation. It's making the cost of inaction concrete. Most people won't act on a problem they intellectually understand. They act when they feel the weight of it.

### Step 3: Solution

Present your solution as the direct answer to the agitated problem. Not as "a" solution—as "the" solution to what you just described.

The transition matters. After agitation, readers are uncomfortable. They want relief. Your solution should arrive as relief.

**Weak transition:**
> "Our consulting services can help you with lead generation."

**Strong transition:**
> "Here's what changes everything: a system that turns the content you're already creating into booked calls. Not more content. Not more traffic. A conversion mechanism that makes your existing work actually pay off."

Notice the structure: acknowledge what they've already tried (content), name what's missing (conversion mechanism), position the solution as completing their existing effort rather than starting over.

## PAS in Practice: Full Example

Here's PAS applied to a coaching offer, showing all three steps as they'd appear in an email:

---

**Subject: Your strategy session problem**

You ran another strategy session yesterday.

Two hours. Good conversation. The prospect was engaged, asked questions, seemed excited about working together.

Then: "Let me think about it." Or "I need to talk to my spouse." Or just silence.

**(That's the Problem: specifically felt, not abstract.)**

Here's what's actually happening. You're giving away your best thinking for free. Every strategy session is a mini-consulting engagement where you demonstrate competence but never create urgency.

Meanwhile, the prospect leaves with enough insight to feel good about the conversation—and no pressure to move forward. They'll "think about it" until the insight fades, and then they're on to the next free session with your competitor.

This is why high-close consultants don't do strategy sessions the way you've been taught. The "give value first" approach optimizes for feeling like a good person. It doesn't optimize for revenue.

**(That's the Agitation: consequences made concrete, competitive framing.)**

The alternative: a sales conversation structured to close on the call.

Not high-pressure tactics. Not manipulation. A framework that moves from problem identification to paid engagement in a single conversation—because you've pre-qualified the prospect and the session itself is designed to create a decision, not defer one.

I built this for my own consulting practice after losing dozens of "great conversations" that went nowhere. Now I teach it to coaches and consultants who are done subsidizing their prospects' research process.

**(That's the Solution: positioned as direct relief from the agitated pain.)**

[Link to the offer]

---

That's 280 words. PAS can expand to 3,000-word sales letters or compress to a three-sentence ad. The structure scales.

## When NOT to Use PAS

PAS fails in specific situations. Understanding these prevents you from forcing the framework where it doesn't fit.

**Don't use PAS when the audience doesn't feel the problem yet.**

PAS requires problem-aware readers. If your audience doesn't recognize they have the problem you're naming, agitation creates confusion, not urgency.

Gene Schwartz's awareness levels explain this: PAS works for problem-aware and solution-aware audiences. For unaware audiences, you need education before persuasion. Use [awareness-level sequencing](/blog/how-to-apply-schwartz-awareness-levels) instead.

**Don't use PAS for sophisticated buyers who've seen it constantly.**

B2B executives, marketing professionals, and anyone who consumes a lot of sales content recognizes PAS structure. When they spot it, they discount it.

For sophisticated audiences, consider [indirect approaches](/blog/direct-response-headlines) that educate first, or narrative structures like [Hook-Story-Offer](/blog/hook-story-offer-blog-posts) that feel less formulaic.

**Don't use PAS when the problem is embarrassing or identity-threatening.**

Weight loss. Debt. Marriage problems. If naming the problem feels like an attack on the reader's competence or character, agitation backfires. They defend instead of engage.

For sensitive problems, lead with aspiration or use [before-after-bridge](/blog/before-after-bridge-blog-posts) to focus on transformation rather than pain.

**Don't use PAS when your solution is preventive, not curative.**

Insurance. Backup software. Estate planning. When the pain hasn't happened yet, agitating a hypothetical problem feels manipulative.

For preventive solutions, future-pacing and logical consequence work better than emotional agitation. Or use fear-based approaches only in moderation, with immediate proof that the threat is real.

## PAS vs. Related Frameworks

**PAS vs. AIDA:** [AIDA](/blog/aida-framework-blog-content) leads with attention-grabbing; PAS leads with problem-naming. Use PAS when the pain point is your strongest hook. Use AIDA when you need to stop the scroll first.

**PAS vs. PASTOR:** [PASTOR](/blog/pastor-framework-blog-posts) is PAS expanded—adding Person, Story, Transformation, Offer, and Response. Use PAS for short-form; PASTOR for long-form sales pages.

**PAS vs. BAB:** [Before-After-Bridge](/blog/before-after-bridge-blog-posts) emphasizes transformation; PAS emphasizes problem. Use BAB for case studies; PAS for diagnostic content.

**PAS vs. 4Ps:** [The 4Ps](/blog/4ps-framework-blog-posts) (Promise, Picture, Proof, Push) is solution-forward; PAS is problem-forward. Use 4Ps when your solution is the unique element. Use PAS when the problem is the unique element.

## Why PAS Persists

Fifty years after mail-order copywriters refined it, PAS remains the default framework because it maps to how humans make decisions.

We don't act on abstract benefits. We act to relieve felt pain. PAS structures that relief.

The practitioners who master it—Kennedy, Carlton, Halbert, Wiebe—all eventually moved beyond it. They use it as foundation, layering sophistication on top. But when they teach, they start here.

If you can only learn one framework, this is it.
