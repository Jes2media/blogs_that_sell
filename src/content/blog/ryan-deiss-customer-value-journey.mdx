Ryan Deiss is the strategist behind DigitalMarketer, one of the most influential marketing education companies of the past decade. His frameworks—particularly the Customer Value Journey—have trained over 15,000 businesses and shaped how modern marketers think about customer acquisition and retention.

Unlike pure copywriters who focus on persuasion mechanics, Deiss operates at the systems level. His contribution isn't a headline formula or email template—it's a complete architecture for how businesses should relate to customers across the entire relationship lifecycle.

Understanding Deiss means understanding marketing as engineering: systematic, measurable, and designed for scale.

## Who Is Ryan Deiss

Deiss started in the mid-2000s selling information products—the classic internet marketing path. But unlike many contemporaries who stayed in the "make money online" niche, he built actual businesses across multiple verticals: survival gear, baby products, DIY crafts, and eventually marketing education.

This multi-business experience shaped his frameworks. He wasn't theorizing about what might work—he was documenting what actually worked across different markets, price points, and customer types.

**DigitalMarketer** launched in 2011 as a membership site teaching these documented systems. It grew into an 8-figure business with certifications, live events, and enterprise consulting. The DM Lab, Traffic & Conversion Summit, and various certification programs trained a generation of digital marketers.

**Key insight from his path:** Deiss built frameworks from practice, not theory. The Customer Value Journey emerged from observing what actually happened when businesses acquired and retained customers—not from imagining what should happen.

His major contributions:

- **Customer Value Journey (CVJ)** — 8-stage relationship mapping
- **Before & After Grid** — Transformation-focused messaging framework
- **The Content Engine** — Systematic content production methodology
- **Customer Avatar Canvas** — Deep audience research framework
- **The Invisible Selling Machine** — Email automation architecture

He's also known for strategic acquisitions (buying companies like Uber Suggest creator Neil Patel's old businesses) and building Scalable, a company focused on acquisition and growth of digital businesses.

## The Customer Value Journey: Complete Framework

The CVJ maps the entire path from stranger to promoter. Unlike simple sales funnels that end at purchase, the CVJ recognizes that the transaction is midpoint, not endpoint.

### The Eight Stages

**Stage 1: Aware**
They discover you exist. This happens through content, ads, referrals, or search. The goal isn't conversion—it's recognition.

**Stage 2: Engage**
They consume your content and begin paying attention. They read a blog post, watch a video, follow on social. Interest is developing.

**Stage 3: Subscribe**
They give permission to contact them. Email signup, SMS opt-in, community membership. They've made a micro-commitment.

**Stage 4: Convert**
First transaction. Often a low-ticket "tripwire" offer—something valuable enough to demonstrate quality but cheap enough to minimize risk.

**Stage 5: Excite**
They experience value and feel good about their decision. This is the critical stage most businesses neglect. Buyer's remorse dies here, or loyalty begins.

**Stage 6: Ascend**
They buy more, upgrade, or purchase higher-ticket offers. The relationship deepens through increased investment.

**Stage 7: Advocate**
They refer others. Not because you asked, but because they genuinely want friends to experience what they experienced.

**Stage 8: Promote**
They actively market for you. Affiliates, ambassadors, case studies, testimonials. They've become an extension of your marketing team.

### The Insight Most Miss

Deiss emphasizes: **most businesses over-invest in stages 1-4 and under-invest in stages 5-8.**

They spend heavily on acquisition, then abandon customers after the sale. But stages 5-8 are where compounding happens:
- Excited customers don't refund
- Ascending customers have higher LTV
- Advocates reduce acquisition costs
- Promoters scale your marketing without ad spend

The CVJ reframes marketing from "how do we get more customers?" to "how do we create customers who create customers?"

## The Before & After Grid

This framework ensures messaging speaks to transformation, not features.

For any offer, map four dimensions:

| Dimension | Before (Current State) | After (Desired State) |
|-----------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Have** | What they have now | What they'll have |
| **Feel** | How they feel now | How they'll feel |
| **Day** | What their day looks like | What their day will look like |
| **Status** | Their current status/identity | Their new status/identity |

**Example for a copywriting course:**

| Dimension | Before | After |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| Have | Scattered notes, no system | Complete framework, templates |
| Feel | Frustrated, unsure | Confident, in control |
| Day | Hours staring at blank page | Writing flows, quick drafts |
| Status | Struggling freelancer | Sought-after copywriter |

**Why this matters for copy:**

Most copy describes the product: "12 modules, 47 videos, PDF workbooks."

Transformation copy describes the After state: "Stop agonizing over every word. Write client-winning copy in half the time."

The Before & After Grid forces you to write about what customers actually want—not what you're selling, but who they become.

## The Customer Avatar Canvas

Deiss's framework for audience research goes deeper than demographics.

**The Canvas includes:**

1. **Goals & Values** — What they're trying to achieve, what they believe matters
2. **Challenges & Pain Points** — What's blocking them, what frustrates them
3. **Sources of Information** — Where they learn, who they trust
4. **Objections & Role in Purchase** — Why they hesitate, who else influences the decision
5. **Demographics** — Age, location, income (the least important section)

**The key insight:** Demographics tell you who they are. Psychographics tell you how to reach them. The Avatar Canvas prioritizes the latter.

When you know their information sources, you know where to advertise. When you know their objections, you know what to address in copy. When you know their values, you know how to position.

## Worked Example: CVJ-Based Email Sequence

Here's how the Customer Value Journey informs a welcome sequence for a marketing SaaS:

---

**Email 1: Engage/Subscribe Confirmation**

Subject: You're in (here's your first quick win)

Welcome to [Product].

Before you dive into the full platform, here's something that takes 3 minutes and shows you exactly what we're about:

[Link to quick tutorial: "Set up your first automated report"]

Most tools make you read documentation for an hour before you see value. We think that's backwards.

Click above, follow the steps, and in 3 minutes you'll have your first automated insight.

Then we'll show you what else is possible.

Talk soon,
[Name]

**Email 2: Excite (Day 2)**

Subject: Did you try it?

Quick check-in.

If you set up that automated report yesterday, you probably noticed something: the data was already there.

That's intentional. We don't make you configure for days before seeing results. The system works out of the box.

Here's what one customer said after her first week: "I spent 4 hours setting up my old tool. With [Product], I had usable data in 10 minutes."

Ready for the next level? Here's a 5-minute tutorial on [next feature]:

[Link]

Each step builds on the last. Keep going.

**Email 3: Ascend (Day 5)**

Subject: You're using 20% of [Product]

You've set up automated reports. You've connected your first integration.

Here's what you haven't seen yet: the features that our power users say actually changed their business.

I'm talking about:
- [Advanced feature 1] — [one-line benefit]
- [Advanced feature 2] — [one-line benefit]
- [Advanced feature 3] — [one-line benefit]

These are included in your plan. You're just not using them yet.

Here's a 15-minute walkthrough showing how one customer uses all three:

[Link to case study video]

If you want hands-on help setting these up, reply to this email. We do onboarding calls for customers who want them.

**Email 4: Advocate (Day 14)**

Subject: Quick favor?

You've been using [Product] for two weeks now.

At this point, you either love it or you're wondering why you signed up. There's rarely an in-between.

If you're in the "love it" camp, I have a small favor:

Would you take 60 seconds to leave a review? [Link]

These reviews help other marketers like you find us. And honestly, they help us understand what's actually working.

If you're in the "why did I sign up" camp, reply and tell me what's not clicking. I read every response, and I'd rather fix the problem than lose you.

Either way, thanks for being here.

---

That's four emails mapping to Engage → Excite → Ascend → Advocate. Each moves the customer to the next CVJ stage.

## When NOT to Use Deiss's Frameworks

The CVJ and related frameworks fail in specific contexts. Understanding these prevents misapplication.

**Don't use the full CVJ for simple, one-time purchases.**

A $12 ebook doesn't need an 8-stage journey. The overhead of building Excite/Ascend/Advocate infrastructure exceeds the customer lifetime value.

For simple products, use straightforward [PAS](/blog/pas-formula-copywriting) or [direct response](/blog/direct-response-content-marketing) without journey mapping.

**Don't use the Before & After Grid when transformation isn't the point.**

Commodity products (office supplies, basic tools) don't create identity transformation. Forcing "status change" language onto mundane purchases sounds absurd.

Use transformation messaging for transformative products. For commodities, focus on convenience, price, or reliability.

**Don't use the CVJ when you can't deliver on stages 5-8.**

If you don't have the resources to create great onboarding (Excite), upsell paths (Ascend), or referral programs (Advocate), mapping those stages creates expectations you'll break.

It's better to have a simple 3-stage journey executed well than an 8-stage journey that falls apart after purchase.

**Don't use the Avatar Canvas as a creativity replacement.**

Some marketers create elaborate fictional personas and never talk to real customers. The Canvas is a research framework, not a creative writing exercise.

If your Avatar isn't based on actual customer conversations, it's fiction—and your copy will feel fictional too.

**Don't use Deiss's frameworks for brand-new markets.**

The CVJ assumes you understand the customer journey. In genuinely new markets, you don't—you're discovering it.

For new markets, use rapid experimentation rather than elaborate journey mapping. Build the CVJ after you understand what actually happens, not before.

## The DigitalMarketer Ecosystem

Understanding where Deiss's frameworks fit in the broader marketing education landscape:

**Deiss vs. Russell Brunson:** Brunson focuses on funnel mechanics—specific page sequences and psychological triggers. Deiss focuses on journey architecture—the overall relationship structure. They're complementary: use Brunson's funnels within Deiss's journey framework.

**Deiss vs. Frank Kern:** Kern emphasizes casual, relationship-based marketing through daily contact. Deiss emphasizes systematic, scalable marketing through automation. Different styles for different businesses.

**Deiss vs. Dan Kennedy:** Kennedy is pure direct response—hard offers, urgent deadlines, immediate conversion. Deiss integrates DR principles into longer relationship journeys. Kennedy for quick conversion, Deiss for lifetime value.

## The Bottom Line

Ryan Deiss's contribution to marketing is systematization. He took the intuitive process of building customer relationships and turned it into engineered, repeatable frameworks.

The Customer Value Journey isn't just a mapping exercise—it's a philosophy: customers are relationships, not transactions. The Before & After Grid isn't just a messaging tool—it's a reminder that people buy transformation, not products.

These frameworks work best for businesses with:
- Products that support ongoing relationships
- Resources to execute post-purchase stages
- Customer lifetime value that justifies journey investment
- Commitment to systematic, measurable marketing

Apply them when scale and sustainability matter more than quick conversion. For pure direct response, simpler frameworks may serve better.

The practitioners who get the most from Deiss understand he's teaching architecture, not tactics. Build the structure right, and the tactics work better inside it.
