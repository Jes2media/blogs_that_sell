Most homepage advice is confidently wrong.

"Put everything above the fold." "Explain your full offering." "Use professional language." "Include something for everyone."

Following this advice produces homepages that are crowded, confusing, and convert nobody.

Here are seven homepage copywriting myths that are probably hurting your business—and what actually works.

---

## Myth 1: Your Homepage Must Explain Everything You Do

**The myth:** Visitors need to understand your complete offering from the homepage. Every service, every product, every capability should be represented.

**Why it's wrong:** Homepages that try to explain everything explain nothing. They overwhelm visitors with options, dilute the core message, and create decision paralysis.

Your homepage has one job: get the right visitors to take the next step.

It's not a brochure. It's not a directory. It's a filter that routes people to where they need to go.

**What actually works:**

Your homepage should communicate three things:
1. **What you do** (in one sentence)
2. **Who it's for** (so the right people self-select)
3. **Why it matters** (the outcome or transformation)

Everything else goes on interior pages.

**Example:**

Bad: "We offer web design, SEO, content marketing, social media management, PPC advertising, email marketing, branding, and video production services for businesses of all sizes."

Good: "We build websites that turn visitors into customers. For service businesses tired of a 'pretty' site that doesn't perform."

---

## Myth 2: The Most Important Content Must Be Above the Fold

**The myth:** If it's not visible without scrolling, visitors won't see it. Cram everything critical into the top 600 pixels.

**Why it's wrong:** People scroll. They've been scrolling for 25 years. The "above the fold" obsession comes from newspaper advertising—which has nothing to do with websites.

What kills conversions isn't content below the fold. It's content above the fold that's so cluttered, confusing, or boring that visitors don't want to scroll.

**What actually works:**

Above the fold, you need:
1. A clear headline that promises value
2. A subhead that clarifies or supports
3. A visual that reinforces the message
4. One obvious CTA

That's it. The job of above-the-fold content is to earn the scroll—not to close the sale.

The rest of your page—proof, features, objection-handling, testimonials—can absolutely live below the fold. Visitors who scroll are more engaged anyway.

---

## Myth 3: Your Homepage Needs to Appeal to Everyone

**The myth:** Because different types of visitors land on your homepage, it should have something for all of them. Enterprise clients, small businesses, individual users—all equally represented.

**Why it's wrong:** Trying to speak to everyone means speaking to no one. Generic messaging doesn't resonate because it doesn't feel personal.

When a visitor lands on your homepage and thinks "this wasn't written for me," they leave.

**What actually works:**

Pick your primary audience and write specifically for them.

If you serve multiple distinct audiences, route them quickly:
- "For Agencies →"
- "For Brands →"

But your main message should target your most valuable or most common visitor type.

**Example:**

Bad: "Solutions for businesses of all sizes—from solo entrepreneurs to Fortune 500 companies."

Good: "Built for marketing agencies tired of cobbling together 6 different tools." (With a smaller link: "Enterprise? Talk to us.")

The specific message that resonates with one group beats the generic message that resonates with none.

---

## Myth 4: Professional Means Formal

**The myth:** To be taken seriously, your homepage copy should sound "professional"—which means formal, corporate, and personality-free.

**Why it's wrong:** "Professional" language is usually jargon-filled, passive, and boring. It signals that you're trying to sound impressive rather than trying to help.

The safest copy is often the least effective copy. It doesn't offend anyone—and it doesn't persuade anyone either.

**What actually works:**

Write like a human talking to another human. Use:
- Active voice, not passive
- "You" and "your," not "clients" and "users"
- Specific language, not abstract jargon
- Personality, not corporate-speak

**Example:**

Formal/boring: "Our organization is committed to providing innovative solutions that enable enterprises to achieve operational excellence."

Human/effective: "We help companies stop wasting money on tools nobody uses. Most clients cut their software costs by 30% in the first quarter."

Professional doesn't mean formal. Professional means clear, competent, and trustworthy.

---

## Myth 5: More Features = More Compelling

**The myth:** If you list more features, integrations, and capabilities, visitors will be more impressed and more likely to buy.

**Why it's wrong:** Long feature lists overwhelm and bore. Visitors don't read them—they skim past them. And when they do read, they can't tell what matters.

Feature lists say "look at all our stuff." Focused messaging says "here's exactly what you need."

**What actually works:**

On your homepage, highlight 3-4 maximum. Choose based on:
- What your best customers care about most
- What differentiates you from alternatives
- What directly ties to outcomes (not capabilities)

Frame features as benefits:
- Not: "AI-powered analytics dashboard"
- But: "See exactly which campaigns are making money"

Save the complete feature list for product or feature pages where people go specifically to compare.

---

## Myth 6: Social Proof Should Be Generic Testimonials

**The myth:** Add some testimonials to your homepage—logos, quotes, star ratings. Any social proof is good social proof.

**Why it's wrong:** Generic social proof is almost useless. "Great company!" and "Would recommend!" don't persuade anyone. Logos from companies visitors don't recognize add nothing.

Weak social proof can actually hurt you—it signals that the best endorsement you could find is underwhelming.

**What actually works:**

Social proof should be:
- **Specific:** Include concrete results or details
- **Relevant:** From customers similar to your target visitor
- **Believable:** Real names, real photos, real companies
- **Objection-handling:** Address what skeptics worry about

**Example:**

Weak: "Amazing service! 5 stars!" – J.S.

Strong: "We switched from HubSpot and cut our marketing software costs by 40%. Took us one afternoon to migrate, and their support team handled the tricky parts." – Sarah Chen, Head of Marketing, Acme Corp (with photo)

Also effective: Specific numbers ("2,000+ companies use us"), specific outcomes ("Average 23% increase in conversion rate"), and credible sources (industry publications, recognizable companies).

---

## Myth 7: Your Homepage Design Should "Wow" Visitors

**The myth:** The homepage needs impressive visuals, animation, and creative design. If it doesn't look amazing, visitors won't respect your brand.

**Why it's wrong:** "Wow" often means slow, confusing, and distracting. Visitors don't come to admire your design—they come to solve a problem or answer a question.

Clever design frequently undermines clear communication. Parallax effects, auto-playing videos, and creative layouts might win design awards while tanking conversions.

**What actually works:**

Homepage design should:
- **Load fast:** Every second of load time costs you visitors
- **Be immediately clear:** No puzzles, no hunting, no interpreting
- **Support the message:** Design serves copy, not the other way around
- **Guide the eye:** Visual hierarchy moves visitors through the page
- **Work on mobile:** Where most of your traffic probably comes from

Simple, fast, and clear beats impressive but confusing every time.

**The test:** Can someone understand what you do and what to do next within 5 seconds? If not, your design is failing.

## What a Converting Homepage Actually Does

Forget the myths. Here's what your homepage should accomplish:

**1. Answer "Am I in the right place?"**
Within 3 seconds, visitors should know if this is for them.

**2. Promise a desirable outcome**
Not features, not process—the end result they want.

**3. Provide enough proof to continue**
Not overwhelming, just enough to keep reading.

**4. Offer one clear next step**
Whether that's "See How It Works," "Start Free Trial," or "Book a Call."

**5. Filter the wrong people out**
The homepage should repel bad-fit visitors as much as it attracts good ones.

That's it. Everything else is optional or belongs on interior pages.

---

## The Quick Audit

Run through these questions for your homepage:

1. Can someone understand what you do in under 5 seconds?
2. Is your target audience obvious from the copy?
3. Is there one clear CTA that stands out?
4. Is your headline about their outcome, not your offering?
5. Is your social proof specific and relevant?
6. Does your page load in under 3 seconds?
7. Does the copy sound like a human wrote it?

If you answered "no" to more than two of these, your homepage is probably costing you business.

---

## The Bottom Line

Homepage myths persist because they feel logical. "Explain everything" sounds thorough. "Appeal to everyone" sounds inclusive. "Sound professional" sounds safe.

But websites don't convert through logic. They convert through clarity, relevance, and trust.

The best homepages are often surprisingly simple. Clear message, clear audience, clear next step. Everything else is noise.

Ignore the myths. Trust simplicity.
