Your treatment proposals sit unopened.

You spent time explaining the recommended treatment, they said they'd "think about it," and then... nothing. They don't call back, don't schedule, don't respond to follow-ups. The case dies in a folder.

You're losing patients to their own procrastination.

## The Real Goal of Sales Letter Copywriting for Dentists

Most dentists think their proposals should document the clinical findings. So they send detailed treatment plans with procedure codes, technical descriptions, and payment options.

Documentation doesn't motivate action. Understanding does.

The real goal: **help patients understand why this treatment matters to their life—not just their teeth—so they take action before the problem gets worse.**

Your follow-up should turn "I'll think about it" into "Let's do this."

[Understanding beats documentation](/blogs-that-sell).

## What Most Dental Proposals Get Wrong

**Mistake #1: Too clinical**

Procedure codes and medical terminology that patients don't understand. Feels like insurance paperwork, not communication.

**Mistake #2: No real urgency**

"Recommended treatment" without explaining what happens if they wait. No motivation to act now.

**Mistake #3: Price shock without context**

A number at the bottom without framing the value or offering manageable payment options.

## The 9 Tips That Actually Move Conversions

### 1. Open with what they shared, not your findings

Reference their specific concerns from the consultation.

**Why it works:** "You mentioned wanting to smile in photos again" connects emotionally. "Treatment plan for case #4072" doesn't.

**Example:**
> "Sarah, I've been thinking about what you said during your visit—that you haven't smiled with your teeth showing in years. I want to help change that. Here's what I recommend..."

---

### 2. Explain the problem in real-world terms

Not "carious lesions." What does it mean for their life?

**Why it works:** Patients don't understand dental terminology. "This cavity will eventually need a root canal or extraction if untreated" is clear and urgent.

**Example:**
> "That tooth in the back right? Right now it's a small cavity—a 20-minute fix. If we wait another year, it'll reach the nerve, and then you're looking at either a root canal or losing the tooth entirely. Much bigger deal, much bigger cost."

---

### 3. Show what happens if they wait

Real consequences, not scare tactics.

**Why it works:** Without understanding what "later" looks like, people choose later. Paint the picture honestly.

| Don't | Do |
|-------|-----|
| "Treatment recommended as soon as possible" | "I know you're thinking of waiting. Here's what I see happen: the crack gets deeper, the tooth becomes sensitive to cold, then hot, then it starts throbbing. Eventually you'll call us on a Saturday with an emergency. I'd rather fix it now when it's simple." |

### 4. Connect treatment to what matters to them

Beyond teeth—their quality of life.

**Why it works:** "Crown on tooth #14" is clinical. "Being able to chew steak on both sides again" is personal.

**Example:**
> "I know you mentioned that you've been avoiding hard foods and always chewing on one side. Once we fix this, you'll be able to eat normally again—without thinking about it. That's what we're really treating here."

---

### 5. Break down the cost with context

Not just the number—what they're paying for.

**Why it works:** "$3,000" feels arbitrary. "$3,000 for a crown that should last 15+ years—about $200/year for not thinking about this tooth again" provides perspective.

| Don't | Do |
|-------|-----|
| "Treatment total: $2,800" | "The total is $2,800. That might sound like a lot—but this crown should last 15-20 years. That's about $15/month for never having to worry about this tooth again. And if we wait until it needs a root canal, we're looking at $4,000+." |

---

### 6. Present payment options clearly

Make it manageable, not overwhelming.

**Why it works:** "$3,000 due at appointment" stops people. "$250/month for 12 months" feels doable.

**Example:**
> "We offer three ways to pay:
> - Pay in full: $2,800 (5% courtesy discount)
> - CareCredit: $242/month for 12 months, 0% interest
> - In-house plan: $350 deposit, then $204/month for 12 months
>
> Pick whatever works for your budget. The important thing is getting this taken care of."

---

### 7. Create real urgency without manipulation

Honest reasons to act now.

**Why it works:** "Limited time offer!" is manipulative. "My schedule gets packed after school starts—if you want a morning appointment, now's the time" is honest.

**Example:**
> "I have two morning appointments open next week—Tuesday at 9am or Thursday at 10am. After that, my next opening is in about 6 weeks. If you want to get this done while it's still straightforward, I'd grab one of those."

See our [guide on ethical urgency](/blog/why-marketing-feels-manipulative) for more.

---

### 8. Make responding easy

Clear next step, multiple options.

**Why it works:** "Contact our office to schedule" is vague. A specific action with specific options gets action.

| Don't | Do |
|-------|-----|
| "Please call us to schedule your appointment" | "Ready to move forward? Here's what to do: Reply to this email with which time works (Tuesday 9am or Thursday 10am), or call us at [number]. Maria will get you scheduled in about 2 minutes." |

---

### 9. Follow up thoughtfully

Don't let the case die from silence.

**Why it works:** One proposal and silence loses cases. A warm follow-up a week later catches people who meant to respond but got busy.

**Example:**
> "Hi Sarah—just checking in. I sent over your treatment plan last week and wanted to make sure it didn't get buried in your inbox. Any questions I can answer? Even if you've decided to wait, I'd love to know so I can update your file."

## FAQ

**How soon should dentists follow up after consultations?**

Within 24-48 hours while the conversation is fresh. After a week, momentum is lost.

**Should dental proposals include insurance breakdowns?**

Yes—show what insurance covers and what the patient's portion is. "Your portion after insurance: $X" is what they need to know.

**How many follow-ups are appropriate?**

Initial proposal, 1-week follow-up, and 1-month check-in. After that, the case is cold—move them to a reactivation list for later outreach.

**Should dentists offer discounts for case acceptance?**

Small courtesy discounts for full payment can help. Avoid steep discounts that devalue your work—they attract price-focused patients who cause problems later.

**How do dentists compete with corporate dental chains?**

Don't compete on price. Compete on relationship, thoroughness, and patient experience. Document your value in the proposal—personal attention, taking time to explain, availability.
