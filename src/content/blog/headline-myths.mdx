Headlines get 80% of the attention in copywriting advice—and 80% of that advice is wrong.

"Always use numbers." "Curiosity gaps work best." "Keep it under 60 characters." "Follow the headline formulas."

Following this advice produces headlines that sound like everyone else's, trick people into clicking, and destroy trust.

Here are seven headline myths that are probably hurting your results—and what actually works.

---

## Myth 1: Numbers Always Win

**The myth:** Headlines with numbers outperform those without. "7 Ways to..." beats "How to..." every time. Always use a number.

**Why it's wrong:** Numbers used to work because they were novel. Now they're everywhere. A quick scroll through any content feed reveals wall-to-wall numbered lists.

When everyone uses numbers, numbers stop differentiating you. "7 Ways to Write Better Headlines" competes with 500 identical articles.

**The real story:**

Numbers signal:
- Specific, scannable content
- A defined scope
- Quick consumption

But they also signal:
- Listicle format (which many readers are tired of)
- Shallow treatment (lists often lack depth)
- The same format as everything else

**What actually works:**

Use numbers when they genuinely serve the content. Don't force them.

**Numbers work for:** Lists, rankings, steps, comparative posts, roundups.

**Numbers don't help:** Deep dives, conceptual pieces, narratives, opinion pieces.

**Alternatives to numbers:**
- "The Complete Guide to..." (signals depth)
- "Why [Surprising Thing] Actually Works" (signals insight)
- "[Problem]? Here's What's Actually Happening" (signals diagnosis)
- "What [Experts/Success Stories] Know About..." (signals authority)

The goal isn't to use numbers or avoid them—it's to match format to content and audience.

## Myth 2: Curiosity Gaps Are the Key to Clicks

**The myth:** Create an "information gap" that can only be satisfied by clicking. Tease without delivering. Leave something unsaid.

**Why it's wrong:** Curiosity gaps work—until you use them for content that doesn't deliver. Then they become manipulation.

**The curiosity gap problem:**
- Readers click expecting value, get fluff
- Trust erodes with every disappointing click
- You train audiences to distrust your headlines
- The technique becomes associated with low-quality content

The worst offenders: "You Won't Believe What Happened Next" and "This One Weird Trick" style headlines that promise much and deliver little.

**What actually works:**

**Curiosity WITH credibility.**

Instead of: "The Secret to Headlines That Work" (vague gap)

Write: "The Headline Formula That Generated 3x More Clicks (And Why Most Get It Wrong)" (specific gap with hint of substance)

**Curiosity that respects the reader:**
- Opens a loop they'll actually want closed
- Suggests real value, not just a gimmick
- Delivers on the promise inside
- Builds trust through consistency

The goal is curiosity that leads to satisfaction, not curiosity that leads to disappointment.

## Myth 3: Headlines Must Be Under 60 Characters for SEO

**The myth:** Google truncates titles over 60 characters, so headlines should stay under that limit. Longer titles hurt SEO.

**Why it's wrong:** Truncation isn't punishment. Google still indexes and understands long titles. The visible portion in search results isn't the whole story.

More importantly, optimizing for character count often means sacrificing clarity or appeal. A compelling 75-character headline will outperform a bland 50-character one.

**The real story:**

Google displays roughly 50-60 characters in search results (varies by device and pixel width). But:
- The full title is still indexed
- Click-through rate depends on what IS visible, not total length
- Many high-ranking pages have longer titles

**What actually works:**

Front-load the important stuff. Put your key value proposition or keywords in the first 50-60 characters, so the visible portion sells the click.

**Example:**

Acceptable: "How to Write Headlines That Convert: The Complete Guide to Copy That Gets Clicked"

The truncated version ("How to Write Headlines That Convert: The Complete...") still works.

**The priority:** Write the best headline first, then consider length. Don't start with a character limit and compromise quality.

## Myth 4: Formulas Are the Key to Great Headlines

**The myth:** Master headline formulas (How to X, X Ways to Y, The Secret to Z) and you'll consistently write winners.

**Why it's wrong:** Formulas are starting points, not endpoints. Over-reliance on formulas produces generic, formulaic headlines that blend into the noise.

**The formula problem:**
- Everyone knows the same formulas
- Formulaic headlines sound like templates
- Formula-first thinking neglects audience understanding
- Formulas optimize for format, not message

When every headline follows "How to [Achieve Desirable Outcome] in [Timeframe]," none of them stand out.

**What actually works:**

Understand what the reader wants and needs first. Then use formulas as tools if they help.

**Better approach:**
1. What does this reader want to achieve or avoid?
2. What makes this content different from alternatives?
3. What's the most compelling way to express that?
4. Does a formula help or hinder?

Sometimes the answer is a formula. Often it's not.

**Example:**

Formula: "7 Ways to Write Better Headlines"

Better: "Your Headlines Sound Like Everyone Else's. Here's Why."

The second isn't a formula—it's a specific, provocative statement that speaks directly to a reader's concern.

## Myth 5: Clever Headlines Are Better Than Clear Headlines

**The myth:** Headlines should be creative, punny, intriguing, and sophisticated. Boring, direct headlines don't capture attention.

**Why it's wrong:** Clever often sacrifices clarity. If the reader doesn't immediately understand the value, they won't click—no matter how witty you are.

**Problems with clever:**
- Puns don't translate across cultures or contexts
- Wordplay can confuse instead of intrigue
- Cleverness often appeals to the writer more than the reader
- Abstract creativity obscures concrete value

The reader has 2 seconds to decide whether to click. "Clever" shouldn't make them work to understand what they're getting.

**What actually works:**

**Clear beats clever almost every time.**

Clever: "The Write Stuff: Penning Your Way to Headline Success"

Clear: "How to Write Headlines That Actually Get Clicked"

The second is "boring" by some standards—but it tells the reader exactly what they'll get.

**When clever works:**
- Your audience expects wit (comedy, entertainment brands)
- The cleverness enhances rather than obscures meaning
- The clever headline is ALSO clear
- You're building a specific brand voice

**Default position:** Start with clear. Add clever only if it doesn't cost clarity.

## Myth 6: Your Headline Should Contain Your Main Keyword

**The myth:** For SEO, the target keyword must appear in the headline. No keyword = no ranking.

**Why it's wrong:** This is partially true but wildly overapplied. Keyword inclusion matters for SEO—but not at the expense of click-worthiness.

**The problems:**
- Forcing keywords creates awkward phrasing
- Keyword-stuffed headlines read like spam
- Google understands context and synonyms
- Click-through rate affects rankings too

A headline that ranks but doesn't get clicked helps no one.

**What actually works:**

**Include your keyword when it flows naturally.** If you can work it in without awkwardness, do so. If it makes the headline worse, use a variation or related phrase.

**Examples:**

Keyword: "email copywriting tips"

Awkward: "Email Copywriting Tips: 7 Email Copywriting Tips for Better Emails"

Natural: "7 Email Copywriting Tips That Actually Boost Open Rates"

Also fine: "Write Emails That Get Opened: A Copywriter's Guide" (related terms, not exact keyword)

Google is smart enough to rank the third headline for "email copywriting tips" if the content is relevant.

**Priority order:**
1. Compelling headline that gets clicked
2. Keyword included naturally
3. Exact keyword match (if possible without harm)

## Myth 7: You Should Always A/B Test Headlines

**The myth:** Test multiple headlines to find the winner. Data beats instinct. Let the audience decide.

**Why it's wrong:** Most headline A/B tests are statistically meaningless and strategically misguided.

**Problems with headline testing:**
- Most content doesn't get enough traffic for significance
- Headlines affect who clicks, not just how many
- Testing small variations yields small insights
- Time spent testing is time not spent creating

Testing "7 Ways" vs "8 Ways" might yield a "winner"—but the insight is useless.

**What actually works:**

**Test headlines when:**
- You have significant traffic (thousands of visitors)
- You're testing substantively different approaches
- The insight applies beyond this one headline
- There's a real hypothesis behind the test

**Better than testing:**
- Study your best-performing headlines
- Analyze competitor headlines in your space
- Understand what your audience responds to
- Develop intuition through practice

**The 80/20:** Spend 80% of effort on writing better headlines, 20% (at most) on testing them.

## What Actually Makes Headlines Work

Forget the myths. Here's what matters:

**1. Immediate clarity**
The reader knows what they're getting within 2 seconds.

**2. Specific value**
Not "improve your marketing" but "get 2x more email opens."

**3. Audience relevance**
Speaks directly to the right reader's concerns.

**4. Believable promise**
Big enough to be interesting, realistic enough to be credible.

**5. Differentiation**
Doesn't sound like the 10 other headlines on the same topic.

A headline that does these five things will outperform one that follows all the "rules."

---

## The Quick Audit

Run through these questions for your headlines:

1. Does this tell the reader exactly what they'll get?
2. Would this stand out among 10 similar articles?
3. Does this sound like a human wrote it (not a template)?
4. Does this make a specific promise you actually deliver on?
5. Would your target reader immediately recognize this is for them?
6. Is this clear enough for someone scanning quickly?
7. Does this work without relying on tricks or manipulation?

If you answered "no" to more than two of these, your headline needs work.

---

## The Bottom Line

Headline myths persist because they offer simple rules for a nuanced skill. Use numbers, create curiosity, follow formulas—these feel like shortcuts.

But great headlines come from understanding what your reader wants and expressing it clearly and compellingly. That requires thinking, not templates.

Ignore the myths. Focus on clarity, specificity, and relevance. Those never go out of style.
