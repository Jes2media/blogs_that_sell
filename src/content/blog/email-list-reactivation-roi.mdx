You have 15,000 subscribers who haven't opened an email in six months. Should you run a reactivation campaign to win them back, or just delete them and move on?

The answer depends on math most operators never do.

Reactivating lapsed subscribers costs 5-7x less than acquiring new ones. A well-run reactivation sequence can return 7:1 on your investment. But a poorly-timed or poorly-targeted reactivation can damage your sender reputation, hurt deliverability to your engaged subscribers, and waste time you could spend on higher-leverage activities.

Here's the framework for deciding.

## The Real Cost of Dead Subscribers

Before calculating the upside of reactivation, understand the ongoing cost of keeping inactive subscribers on your list.

**Direct costs:** Most ESPs charge by subscriber count. 15,000 inactive subscribers at $0.003/subscriber/month is $45/month, $540/year. Not catastrophic, but not nothing.

**Indirect costs:** This is where it gets expensive. ISPs like Gmail and Yahoo track engagement. When you send to 50,000 subscribers and only 20,000 engage, you're telling Gmail that 60% of your audience doesn't want your emails. Over time, this tanks your deliverability to everyone, including your best customers.

The deliverability damage is hard to quantify, but it's real. A 5% drop in inbox placement across your engaged audience costs more than the $540 you're paying to keep the dead subscribers around.

**The baseline decision:** If you're not going to reactivate them, remove them. The cost of keeping non-engaged subscribers exceeds the cost of removing them in almost every scenario.

## The Reactivation Math

A successful reactivation campaign brings back 10-20% of targeted subscribers, according to [Klaviyo's 2025 benchmark data](https://www.klaviyo.com/). That's for multi-email sequences with proper segmentation. Single-email attempts average only 6%.

Let's run the numbers on your 15,000 inactive subscribers.

**Conservative scenario (10% reactivation):**
- 1,500 subscribers return to active status
- If your revenue per active subscriber is $2/month, that's $3,000/month recovered
- Annual value: $36,000

**Optimistic scenario (20% reactivation):**
- 3,000 subscribers return
- At $2/subscriber/month: $6,000/month recovered
- Annual value: $72,000

**Cost to run the campaign:**
- 4-email sequence to 15,000 subscribers: minimal (you're already paying for the ESP)
- Time to write and set up: 2-4 hours
- Potential deliverability risk: real, but manageable with proper execution

The ROI math usually favors reactivation. But the math changes based on three variables: how long they've been inactive, how they became subscribers, and what you're selling.

## When Reactivation Works

Reactivation has high ROI when these conditions are true.

**They went inactive recently.** [Win-back emails sent 30-45 days after last engagement](https://www.klaviyo.com/) achieve 2.3x higher conversion than emails sent at 90+ days. The longer someone has been inactive, the lower your odds of bringing them back. Six months of inactivity is recoverable. Two years is probably not.

**They were real subscribers to begin with.** People who opted in for a specific lead magnet, attended a webinar, or purchased previously have a relationship with you. They remember who you are. Reactivation is reminding them, not introducing yourself.

If your inactive subscribers came from a giveaway, a co-registration, or a purchased list, reactivation won't work. They were never really your subscribers. They gave you an email address, not their attention.

**You have something new to offer.** The best reactivation hook is "something has changed." A new product, a new approach, a major update to something they previously showed interest in. If nothing has changed, why would they suddenly re-engage?

"We miss you" emails without a compelling reason to return have low response rates. "We just launched X and thought you'd want to know" gives them a reason.

**Your engaged list is healthy.** Reactivation campaigns temporarily hurt your engagement metrics. You're intentionally emailing people who don't open. If your deliverability is already borderline, a reactivation campaign can push you over the edge.

Run reactivation when your core metrics are strong and you can absorb a short-term hit.

## When Reactivation Is a Waste

Skip reactivation and just clean the list when these conditions apply.

**They've been inactive for 18+ months.** At this point, many email addresses are abandoned, forwarded, or converted to spam traps. The deliverability risk outweighs the potential recovery. According to [industry data](https://www.zetaglobal.com/), email addresses inactive for 18+ months have a spam trap rate 3-4x higher than recently inactive addresses.

**You don't know how they got on your list.** If you can't identify the lead magnet or entry point, they probably came from a low-quality source. Subscribers with unknown origins have the lowest reactivation rates and highest spam complaint rates.

**Your deliverability is already struggling.** If you're seeing declining open rates across your engaged segments, fix that first. Adding a reactivation campaign to a deliverability problem makes the problem worse.

**The list segment is too small to matter.** Reactivating 200 subscribers isn't worth the time. Even at 20% success, you're recovering 40 people. Focus on growth instead.

**You have nothing new to say.** If your offer, content, and approach are identical to when they stopped engaging, why would reactivation work? They left for a reason. Without something different, you're just annoying them.

## The Reactivation Sequence That Works

If you decide to reactivate, here's the structure that performs.

**Email 1 (Day 1): The pattern interrupt.** Subject line signals something is different. Not "We miss you" but "Quick question" or "Should I remove you?" Open with acknowledgment: "You haven't opened an email from me in a while. I get it, inboxes are overwhelming." Then ask a direct question or make a direct offer.

**Email 2 (Day 3): The value reminder.** Remind them why they subscribed. What problem do you solve? What have they missed? Link to your best recent content or your most compelling offer. This is the email where you re-sell the relationship.

**Email 3 (Day 5): The incentive.** If you have a paid product, offer a discount they can't get elsewhere. "I'm holding this 30% discount for subscribers who've been on the list for a while." Time-limit it. If you don't sell products, offer exclusive content or access.

**Email 4 (Day 7): The final notice.** "I'm cleaning my list tomorrow. If you want to stay, click here. If not, no hard feelings." This email consistently has the highest engagement because it triggers loss aversion. People who don't click get removed.

[Klaviyo's data shows](https://www.klaviyo.com/) that 3-4 email sequences outperform single-email win-back attempts by 2-3x. The sequence gives people multiple chances to re-engage and multiple reasons to do so.

**After the sequence:** Anyone who doesn't engage gets removed. No exceptions. The point of reactivation isn't to keep everyone, it's to identify who's worth keeping.

## The Decision Framework

Run through this checklist before launching a reactivation campaign.

**Step 1: Segment by inactivity duration.**
- 30-90 days inactive: High-value reactivation targets
- 90-180 days inactive: Medium-value, worth attempting
- 180-365 days inactive: Low-value, be selective
- 365+ days inactive: Consider skipping, just remove

**Step 2: Segment by source quality.**
- Known lead magnet or purchase: Worth reactivating
- Unknown source or bulk import: Skip, remove directly

**Step 3: Check your current deliverability.**
- Open rates above 25% on engaged segments: Safe to run reactivation
- Open rates below 20%: Fix deliverability first, then reactivate

**Step 4: Calculate the upside.**
- (Inactive subscribers) × (10% reactivation rate) × ($2/subscriber/month) × (12 months) = Annual value recovered
- If the number is meaningful relative to your business, proceed
- If it's trivial, skip reactivation and just clean

**Step 5: Confirm you have something to say.**
- New product, new offer, or compelling content: Proceed
- Nothing new: Either find an angle or skip it

## The Benchmark to Hit

A successful reactivation campaign should recover 10-15% of targeted subscribers. Below 5% suggests your list was too far gone or your sequence wasn't compelling. Above 20% means you're either excellent at reactivation or your "inactive" threshold was too aggressive.

Track not just reactivation rate but subsequent engagement. If 15% reactivate but 80% of them go inactive again within 60 days, your reactivation was a temporary win, not a real recovery.

The subscribers worth keeping are those who return and stay engaged. Measure success at 90 days, not day 7.

## When This Isn't the Problem

Sometimes the question isn't whether to reactivate. It's why you have so many inactive subscribers in the first place.

**If more than 30% of your list is inactive:** Your problem is upstream. Either your lead magnet attracts the wrong people, your content doesn't match what they signed up for, or you're emailing too infrequently to stay top of mind.

**If subscribers go inactive quickly:** If people disengage within 30-60 days of subscribing, your welcome sequence and early emails aren't delivering on the promise of your opt-in. Fix the onboarding before worrying about reactivation.

**If your list never grows:** [60-70% of first-time buyers never return within 90 days](https://www.shopify.com/), according to Shopify's retention data. Some attrition is normal. But if your list is shrinking because inactive removals exceed new subscribers, focus on growth and onboarding, not reactivation.

Reactivation is a useful tool, but it's not a strategy. It recovers value you've already lost. The higher-leverage move is fixing whatever caused the disengagement.
